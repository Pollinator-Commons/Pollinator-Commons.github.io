Do landscapes shape the emergence of institutions governing the pollinator commons?

An international research collaboration studying pollinator-dependent smallholder farming in the Western Himalayas and Central New York — and the collective action needed to protect the wild bees that sustain them.

About the Project

Wild insect pollinator declines are threatening both food production and ecosystem integrity worldwide. For smallholder farmers of pollinator-dependent crops, this decline can threaten already precarious livelihoods. Despite urgent need, there is remarkably little evidence of collective action institutions governing wild pollinators — this project asks why, and what it would take to change that.

The Problem

Apple farming communities in both the Western Himalayas and Central New York depend on wild bee pollination services, yet those services are declining. The problem has been framed as a "tragedy of the commons," yet existing top-down interventions have not been effective. Meanwhile, the spatial scale at which pollinators operate does not match the scale at which current institutions govern land use — leaving pollinators ungoverned.

This creates a novel commons problem: a shared resource that is mobile, cryptic, and whose boundaries do not align neatly with property or jurisdictional boundaries.

Our Approach

We use a structured comparison between Uttarakhand, India and Central New York, USA — two regions that share the apple-growing context but differ in biophysical character, land-use dynamics, and existing institutions. This comparative design allows us to isolate the spatial, social, and environmental conditions under which collective action might emerge.

Study Sites

🏔️ Mukteshwar, Uttarakhand, India

60 smallholder apple farms across an elevation gradient in the lower Himalayas, in collaboration with the Center for Ecology Development and Research (CEDAR). Apple yields in Uttarakhand are the lowest among India's major apple-producing states, and pollination limitation has been documented as a significant driver.

🍎 Central New York & Hudson Valley, USA

18+ apple farms in New York State, with parallel ecological sampling (pan traps, pollination exclusion experiments, forest surveys) and a statewide farmer survey of over 3,000 farms to assess collective action potential.

Core Research Goals

Goal 1

Examine how on-farm practices interact with landscape context to influence pollinator communities

Goal 2

Examine how spatial variation in social, institutional, and environmental context influences farmers' interest and ability to engage in collective action

Goal 3

Determine the threshold of spatial extent and farm configuration necessary to generate positive feedbacks that maintain sufficient pollinator supply

Research

Our work spans three integrated research questions, combining field ecology, social science, remote sensing, and computational modeling.

Research Question 1

How does landscape context interact with on-farm practices to shape pollinator supply?

  • Pollinator community sampling — Pan traps and hand-netting at 60 farms in Uttarakhand and 18+ farms in New York across bloom and post-bloom periods
  • Pollination exclusion experiments — Quantifying the degree of pollen limitation on apple production at both sites
  • Forest structure mapping — LiDAR scanning of forest plots adjacent to orchards to assess how forest structure drives pollinator communities
  • Land use / land cover mapping — Sentinel-2 satellite imagery and random forest classification to map landscape composition within 500m buffers around farms
  • Camera traps — Deployed across farm sites in India and New York to document pollinator visitation
Research Question 2

How do social, institutional, and environmental landscapes influence farmers' interest in collective action?

  • Household surveys — Baseline surveys with 60 farmers in Uttarakhand examining knowledge of pollinator dependence, perceptions of decline, and willingness to engage in conservation
  • Statewide farmer survey — Mail survey distributed to over 3,000 farmers across New York State assessing awareness of wild pollinators and collective action potential
  • Qualitative interviews — In-depth interviews exploring farmer decision-making and institutional constraints
  • Social network analysis — Characterizing individual and landscape-scale patterns in propensity for collective action
Research Question 3

What spatial configurations would generate positive feedbacks sufficient to sustain pollinator supply?

  • Agent-based model (ABM) — Simulating the emergence of collective action institutions under different spatial conditions, with development led by Co-PI Williamson
  • Scenario generation — Working with stakeholders to generate alternative future landscape scenarios
  • Threshold analysis — Determining the spatial extent and farm configuration needed for cooperative management to become self-sustaining

News & Updates

November 2026

Forest Structure LiDAR Fieldwork — Ithaca, NY

Postdoc Ryan Blackburn led fieldwork to collect LiDAR measurements at forest sites adjacent to apple orchards in Ithaca, NY. This work adds to datasets from 10 newly established orchard-adjacent forest plots to examine how forest structure shapes pollinator communities.

August 2026

ESA Annual Meeting Presentation

Ryan Blackburn and co-authors presented findings on how subcanopy forest structure drives pollinator community composition in New York forests at the Ecological Society of America Annual Meeting in Salt Lake City, UT.

Summer 2026

Year 2 Fieldwork Complete — New York

The team deployed 22 camera traps across 11 NY farms, completed pan trap and pollination exclusion sampling at 18 farms, and established 10 new forest sites. The statewide farmer survey was distributed to over 3,000 NY farmers.

June 2026

Project Presentation at UPES, Dehradun

PIs Allington, Singh, Williamson, and Luthra presented the project's goals, methods, and Year 1–2 findings at the University of Petroleum and Energy Studies (UPES) in Dehradun. Discussions included the potential for UPES to serve as an institutional home for the project's insect specimen collection.
